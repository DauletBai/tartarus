The Archaic Lexical Corpus

The archaic lists, excavated in Uruk and dated to about 3,200 BCE, contain lists of words and expressions that document the earliest writing system in the world. Most archaic lexical lists are standardized so that their contents may be reconstructed from multiple partially preserved duplicates. In addition, the archaic lexical corpus was transmitted to the third millennium with only minor adaptations and was faithfully copied for many centuries. These later copies are often very helpful in reconstructing and interpreting their early ancestors.

The archaic lexical lists, published in an exemplary fashion by Englund and Nissen in 1993, were teaching tools (and perhaps reference works) that serve to disseminate the new administrative technique known as writing in a standardized fashion in order to optimize the efficiency of the system. In the following we will compare the contents of the lexical corpus to the repertory of words and signs in use in the archaic administrative documents. It will become apparent that the lists closely adhere to the needs and purposes of the officials for whom writing was invented. At the same time, however, the lists open the technique of writing to intellectual and ideological explorations and thus go beyond the initial purpose of administrative recording.

1. An Example

An example of an archaic lexical tablet is W 11986,a (Englund and Nissen 1993, plate 4). The obverse preserves eleven lines from the list of professions called Lu A. As in all lexical texts of the period, each line is preceded by a single impression of the stylus, which may simply mean “new item,” in this context and is transliterated below as ¶. W 11986,a is an unusual exemplar in that it has an extract from the lexical list of metals on the reverse.

The obverse contains the following text:

column 1:  
1' [¶] ´nam2` di supreme judge?
2' ¶ nam2 nam2 ?
3' ¶ nam iria1 mayor?
4' ¶ nam erin lord of the cedar?
5' ¶ gal ´šubur` overseer of ...
   
column 2:  
1' ¶ ´abgal` sage (a kind of priest?)
2' ¶ ´kingal` chief
3' ¶ tiru (GALa.TE) courtier
4' ¶ gala-sukkal overseer of the messengers
5' ¶ gadaa sukkal ?
6' ¶ gala gaa ?

Several of the lines transliterated and translated above are damaged on the original. The list Lu A, however, is attested in numerous exemplars that duplicate each other so that the reconstructions are not in any doubt. The lines preserved on this fragment represent the items 3-7 and 15-20 of the composite text.

The readings of the signs and the translations are all mainly based on evidence from much later texts and may prove to be radically wrong. For several of the titles enumerated here we do not have context attestations that could elucidate their actual responsibilities, so that in most cases we are reduced to etymological reasoning. The sign iria1 (column 1, line 3'), for instance, represents the word for city, and nam2 is an early word for a high official (or perhaps “lord”). That the combination of those two signs means “mayor” and is to be read nam-iri is plausible but by no means assured. Similarly, Old Babylonian and later lexical texts indicate the reading tiru or tirum, translated as “courtier.”, for the sign combination GAL.TE (column 2, line 3'). In applying this reading and translation to an archaic text, we make a leap of about one and a half millennia back in time. There is little or no guarantee that this is indeed what the scribes in late fourth millennium Uruk had in mind.

Lu A and the other lexical texts were, first of all, teaching tools that provided the correct way to enter a variety of words in texts. In addition, some of these lists go on to catalogue a full inventory of words within a semantic field, e.g., professions, birds, fish, and thus contain knowledge of a rather different kind: knowledge of language and knowledge of the world.

In the discussion that follows, we will first investigate the very close relationship between the archaic lexical corpus and the administrative texts for which the writing system was originally invented (§2). Then we will explore why many of the items in the lexical texts never appear anywhere else and whether the lexical texts, therefore, have a meaning and use independent of their practical utility in the bureaucracy (§3). In the conclusion, we will compare the evidence for the practical and the theoretical aspects of the corpus.

2. Lexical Lists and the Archaic Administrative Corpus

There can be little doubt that the earliest writing system was invented for the purpose of recording administrative transactions. The great majority of archaic texts are of an administrative nature, e.g., accounting for the deliveries of animals, grain, or metals, recording the work on arable land, or balancing the deliveries of grain with the production of beer.

The texts date to the late Uruk period (c. 3500-3100 B.C.), a period of strong urbanization in which the city of Uruk had expanded to an area of about 100 hectares, much larger than any comparable site of the period. Urban expansion necessitated an economic structure of exchange and redistribution, marking the end of relatively self-sufficient communities. While the cities were strong in political, symbolic, and economic power, they depended heavily on the surrounding country for labor force and for the production of food. The emergence of an urban elite called for the import of luxury items to symbolize their status in monumental buildings, expensive furniture, or jewelry. This produced a class of specialist craftsmen, stone workers, metal workers and the like, who need to be supported in return for their skills.

The complex processes of urbanization, social differentiation, specialization, and the growing mutual dependency between city and countryside cannot be properly described here; for an overview one may consult Van de Mieroop 1997. For our present argument it is sufficient to say that the emergence of writing was a response to the increasing complexity of the economy and administration of Uruk in the late fourth millennium. The earliest texts, therefore, do not record the myths and legends of the time but rather the transactions of urban institutions.

In documenting transactions, these early documents have a limited scope and a limited use for language. While they need to record words for, say, “sheep,” and “grain,” there is very little or no use for actual sentences or even for verbs. The main elements of the texts are dictated by the relevant aspects of a transaction: numbers, commodities, indicators of time and place, identification of individuals. The relations between these various elements are not indicated by linguistic means but mainly by the placement of the words or signs on the tablet. The syntax, in other words, is provided by the layout and largely determined by the structure of the transaction not by the sentence structure in any existing vernacular of the time (Green 1981).

MSVO 3, 51 (from Englund 1998, p. 187)

According to the above text, two different parties each delivered barley groats and malt for beer production (two columns on the right). The third column from the right contains intermediate calculations, representing the total amounts of barley groats (upper case) and malt (lower case). The final (left-most) column contains the grand total. The bottom case holds the name of the official in charge, an individual well known from several archaic account texts. The relationships between the entries are expressed by the arrows in the schematic representation of the tablet. On the original tablet, these relationships are not indicated explicitly in full sentences but rather by the spatial arrangement of the tablet.

It is against this background that we may understand the kinds of signs and words that are – or are not – listed in the lexical corpus. Several of the lists are thematically organized and enumerate the whole gamut of possible or potential commodities that a scribe might need to record. Two lists provide entries for the identification of individuals: a list of professions, traditionally titled Lu A, and the list known as “Officials” that probably contains a mix of personal names and professions. A list of cities and a geographical list provide the terms for indicating places:

Several of these lists (wood, birds, fish) utilize determinatives that indicate the general semantic range of a word. Thus, names of fish and names of birds are followed by the signs MUŠEN and KU6, respectively, and trees and wooden objects are preceded by the sign GIŠ. MUŠEN, KU6, and GIŠ are also used as the general words for bird, fish, and tree and, in some cases, these determinatives were pronounced and were part of the word (as in English “apple tree,” where the word tree is part of the name of a kind of tree). In other cases, however, determinatives are exclusively a matter of writing, bearing no relation to the form of the word. Determinatives were part of the writing system from the very beginning, and the use of these determinatives in the organization of some of the archaic lists demonstrates once again the close affinity between the lexical corpus and the structure of the newly invented writing system.

Several important aspects of the writing system are not included in any of the thematic word lists but are found in the enigmatic composition now labeled “Word List C,” sometimes mistakenly called the “Tribute List.” A section of this text, preserved in 57 archaic exemplars, reads as follows (lines 3-26; variants are minimal and are disregarded):

5 munu3(?)     5 units of salt(?)
5 gazi     5 units of mustard
5 ŠA2x(NE.E2)     5 ?
5 uza     5 ducks
5 maš si4a     5 brown goats
10 tub     10 long-tailed sheep(?)
10 gukkalc     10 fat-tailed sheep
1 ab2 KALa     1 ... cow
1 amar-gab     1 milk calf

10 kar2a     10 ?

Much is very uncertain here, but we may discern food items, a number of domestic animals and their products, a bird (the duck), perhaps a fish (suhur), and eggs (the interpretation of this line depends heavily on later versions of this list; see below).

This is clearly not a thematic lexical list but rather an elementary exercise that allowed the student to go over those terms and expressions that would be needed most by a scribe. While the thematic lists indulge in enumerating as many professions, birds, fish, trees, and vessels as possible, including obscure ones and those never found in other contexts, Word List C mainly contains items that are well known from administrative texts. Moreover, these items are combined with numbers, just as in actual records. The section quoted above is repeated verbatim (lines 31-54), which has led to the interpretation of the composition as a piece of literature, or even as secret knowledge (see most recently Goodnick Westenholz 1998). If we look at this composition as an early exercise, such explanations seem unnecessary. The repeated section is the passage with numbers and number writing so vital for the use of this system that it makes a lot of sense to repeat it. There may be some intrinsic meaning to this repetition as well. If so, it is lost to us. In the following sections of the composition, we recognize words that deal with labor on the land, including tree, reed, water, hoe and plow. Since the administration of agricultural workers is another important aspect of archaic bookkeeping, this confirms our interpretation of Word List C as a primer on record keeping.

Word List C allowed the student to combine numbers with nouns in what appear to be realistic example entries. Most interesting is the last item in the passage quoted above, which perhaps means "61 eggs." Later versions of the text have “71 eggs of the kad4 bird” (an unidentified bird). In cuneiform, the significance of the number 71 is immediately clear: it is the combination of the three most basic number signs in the sexagesimal system: 60 + 10 + 1. The archaic text reads 61, combining only two such signs – one variant exemplar (W 21208, 2), however, sides with the later versions and has the number 71. We may venture the conclusion that the number was chosen for its educational value.


W 21208,2: Word List C

Two lists comparable to Word List C are Word List D (also called “Food” or “Grain”; see Civil 1984) and the Plant list (see Civil and Biggs 1966). These two lists are not obviously thematic in nature and contain further important terms relevant for writing administrative documents. Unlike Word List C, however, they are relatively rare. Word List D includes a list of basic numbers (reconstructed from later parallels) as well as various food items, including grain and meat products. The list of Plants contains a thematic section on plants but also has sections on divisions of the day (see Englund 1988: 164-168) and other subjects, unfortunately poorly understood.

Finally, the corpus of archaic lexical lists contains various non-standardized list that are not duplicated and are not found in later recensions. These lists, including two swine lists, are important because they demonstrate the trial-and-error aspect of lexicography in the archaic period. Not every exercise made it into the standard curriculum; some were abandoned never to be used again.


W 12139: List of Swine

The archaic writing system is basically an administrative tool, and therefore, numbers and number writing are crucial for anybody who needs to learn to use this system. Archaic metrology is very complex, using 5 basic systems and several derived ones. The five basic systems are used for counting different types of things, such as discrete objects, days, or areas of land. In some cases the different systems use the same signs for different values. In spite of their complexity and importance, numbers are not treated to any large extent in the archaic lexical corpus. We have seen that the Word Lists C and D pay some attention to numbers, but this stands in no relation to the significance of number writing in archaic record-keeping. To some extent this may be explained by the very importance and ubiquity of numbers. The metrological systems that seem so complicated to the modern observer were in common use and were familiar to everybody–even those outside the circles of scribal specialists. Numbers are not difficult to draw, they are rather straightforward in design, and their proper use was easier to learn through accounting exercises (several of which have been identified) than through lists (see further Englund 1998, 106-110).

W 19408,76: Obverse and reverse both contain computations of the surface areas of two very large fields. The measurements are different, but both add up to exactly 10 šar2, or about 39 km2. The unrealistically large figures and the identical round number as end result identify this tablet as an exercise (see Englund 1998: 110 and 210).

The archaic lexical corpus lists the terms needed for the administrative texts for which the new writing technique was designed. They are not, as has sometimes been alleged, secret knowledge, and they are not theological in nature. They do not describe the proper order of the world as designed by the gods. They are also not based upon pre-existing oral compositions. Interpretations along such lines, which go back to Von Soden 1936, fail to recognize the very limited nature of early writing. It is administrative in character in use as well as in design. The lexical tradition is born from the necessity to standardize and teach a technique that was developed to record transactions. That this is indeed the case may also be argued from negative evidence. Those aspects of reality and those parts of the lexicon that were not relevant for the Uruk bureaucracy are not found in the lexical corpus. There is no list of stars, no list of gods, no list of wild animals, and no list of rivers.

3. The Vocabulary of the Thematic Lists

While in the preceding we have argued for the close relationship between the practice of writing and the earliest lexical lists, even the most superficial inspection of these lists will indicate that that is only true on a very general level. Birds are attested in the administrative record and therefore there is a list of birds. Very few of these birds listed, however, are ever found outside of the lexical corpus. The same applies to the list of fish, the list of vessels and clothing, and the list of professions (Lu A). In other words, while the kinds of words in lexical and administrative records are the same, the actual vocabulary of the lexical texts is much larger. In order to discuss this discrepancy we need to take a closer look at some of the individual lists, in particular the thematic lists.

The list of professions, Lu A, which includes about 125 items, is the most frequently attested archaic lexical composition with at least 164 exemplars. It is likely that the list contains the names of professions in contemporary Uruk in a more or less hierarchical order. The composition begins with the word nam2 šiš-šita, which, in a much later vocabulary, is translated as king (see MSL 14, 248: 36-37 and MSL 12, 93: 26). Other identifiable words refer to the “lord of the city” (nam2 iria1, line 5), to various priestly functions (for instance enkum and ninkum in 67-68) and to a variety of supervisors (gala) of all possible professions. Of all these professions, only a very small minority is found in administrative texts, and even those titles that are attested are very rare (see Englund 1998: 103-106). The attested terms demonstrate that these professions did indeed exist in contemporary Uruk. The absence of most of these titles from the administrative record may be explained by the restricted nature of this record. Only those officials who were directly involved with the types of transactions for which writing was used would be found in administrative texts.

A very similar picture arises from the lists of birds and the list of fish. The length of these two lists cannot be established with certainty because of the fragmentary nature of the sources. Judging by later parallels, however, each of these lists may have had over a hundred different names. Deliveries of birds and fish in the administrative record are restricted to a few species. Texts dealing with fish, for instance, most frequently record ku6a (fresh fish) and suhur (dried fish); a few other types of fish appear more rarely. In addition, the fish records contain a variety of signs that consist of the sign GA2a inscribed with other signs (KU6a, SUHUR, UD, HI) and apparently denoting a container with fish or processed fish products. These words also appear in the fish list, so that this list includes not only natural species but also terms related to fisheries. Still, the great majority of items in the Fish list are otherwise unknown and seem superfluous from a practical point of view.


W 20593,7: Fragmentary exemplar of the archaic fish list. The third column has various entries that consist of the sign GA2a, inscribed with other signs: GA2a × UD, GA2a × PAP; GA2a × A; GA2a × KU6a

The lists of professions, birds, and fish, therefore, may not be entirely explained from the utilitarian perspective of providing all signs that a future scribe might need to know. There is an apparent urge to be complete, to list all officials, birds, and fish, irrespective of their relevance in administrative records. The reason for this should first be sought in the project of writing itself. A new administrative tool is of little use if it fails to be able to deal with all the tasks it is designed for. Designing such a recording system may be compared to designing a database or a paper form. Such design involves analyzing the data that this database or form are supposed to hold –not only the common types, but all of them, so that every possibility is covered –or else the project will be considered a failure. Those who designed this writing system had no example to follow, no handbook of administrative systems’ design, and yet they managed to create a very successful tool that, in its basics, remained in use for over three millennia. The overkill in listing professional names, fish, birds, and other terms has a very good systemic reason. Those words that do not exist within the system from the outset may need to be invented later on, and the more the system gets tampered with, the less it will be standardized, and the less effective it is. The fact that a nam2 di (supreme judge?) might take responsibility for a written transaction is enough to give him a place in one of the handbooks of writing. If Word List C and other comparable lists are the primers, or “Writing for Dummies,”, the thematic lists are the “Writing Handbooks,” with all the obscure and rarely or never used options one expects in full documentation.

It is useful, in this context, to include in the discussion the list of Vessels and Textiles, a list attested in about 100 exemplars. The Vessels section of this list contains a long passage in which every entry consists of the sign DUGb inscribed with another sign. The sign DUGb represents a vessel, and with some commodity inscribed, it presumably denotes a vessel filled with that commodity. Most of these complex signs are never attested outside of the Vessels List and have been described as “theoretical signs” (Krispijn 1992). This designation is very apposite indeed because the signs illustrate a well-known and frequently used technique that was part and parcel of the writing system. While the actual signs may have been less relevant for the aspiring scribe, the practice of writing one sign inside another one was important enough as a productive aspect of the system.

4. The Nature of the Archaic Lexical Lists

While the general comparison between the kinds of words in administrative and lexical texts uncovered a great commonality (§2), the more detailed comparison revealed that the vocabulary in the lists is much larger and includes many words and signs that were never used in the practice of administrative recording (§3). It turns out that, for a balanced assessment of the nature of this corpus, we must distinguish between at least two groups of texts. The Word Lists C and D and the list of Plants seem to be rather close to the actual administrative practice and may have been designed with this practice in mind. The thematic lists, the list of Professions (Lu A), Birds, Fish, Vessels and Textiles, Wood, Animals, Cities, and Metals, cast their net much wider and seem to represent an attempt to list all words known within those semantic fields. It is possible that within this group we may make even more distinctions. The list of Animals, for instance, is build around a small set of animal words, each provided with a fixed set of qualifications, apparently representing a full set of animal categories. With our limited understanding of the contents of these lists, however, we can only get so far.

In the preceding, we have explained the exaggerated inclusiveness of the thematic lists by pointing at the necessity for an administrative system to be complete and to be able to handle everything. Yet, this cannot be the whole story. It is very likely that the list of professions, Lu A, was organized along hierarchical lines. Copying this list, by far the most frequently attested list in the archaic record, reconfirmed and thus implicitly justified the order of the society that the aspiring scribe was going to serve. Listing as many bird or fish names as possible and coming up with writings for each of these words –quite apart from their actual relevance in writing practice –has an intellectual value and must have given intellectual satisfaction. The project of listing every word that might come up in a transaction may well have been coupled with such ideological and intellectual ends.

The archaic lexical texts represent first of all the manuals of a new administrative tool. Second, they open the door to intellectual and ideological explorations with only a nominal link to the bureaucratic technique. The late fourth millennium lexical lists (with the exception of the Wood list) were transmitted into the third millennium. Several were faithfully copied for more than one and a half millennia, all the way to the Old Babylonian period (c. 2000-1600 B.C.). Their initial practical value as manuals was soon forced to the background as writing developed and the language changed. They became tokens of archaic and arcane knowledge, copied by scribes wanting to exhibit their familiarity with ancient tradition.
